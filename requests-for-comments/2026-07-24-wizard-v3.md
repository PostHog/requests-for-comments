## Some historical context

## What makes a Wizard V3? (What we need to build next)

Wizard V3 is marked by a better integration through engaging the user while it works. I don't think we'll continue to be appealing as a static Wizard in a few more quarters. Quality models who can do a better job is getting cheaper and more available every day.

Where we standout in 6 months is by focusing on what a generic model cannot do:
- We understand PostHog and self-driving better than any model. It's a paradigm. We're here to teach.
- Good content to build conceptual understanding. We're here to help the user understand what to ask and what they can ask. The human still needs conceptual understanding.
- We're here to be a mirror. We're here to help someone reason about their business, not just their data. What matters to them dictates what they need to track and instrument.


To do this, we have: 

0. Composable microskills — the substrate everything else sits on
1. Self-driving first experience
2. Customizable, modular setup
3. Our edge is not the model
4. Higher instrumentation quality

### 0. Composable microskills (we'll build this in Wizard V2.5)

This one is first because the other four are downstream of it.

We decompose to mini skills, something the orchestrator can work with, and we make it trivial to reorganize what an agent does. Once we have the microskills we need, we can experiment with outputs all day. Custom input, BYOA context packages, better quality from more compact context — all of those are shapes we can try cheaply once the substrate exists, and all of them are expensive as long as we're editing one monolithic skill set.

Two things I believe about the shape of that context:

- **It can look more and more like what we know are good docs.** GPT 5.6 is really good at reasoning through limited ambiguity now. We don't have to over-specify.
- **Examples are becoming shape anchors, not exemplars.** Fewer of them, smaller, there to establish form rather than to be copied.

That's a real change from what we ship today, which is docs plus skills plus packaged examples plus context, all of it sized for models that needed hand holding.

Worth its own investigation: the shape of good context is moving with every model generation, and if we rebuild the skill set for today's models without understanding that trajectory, we'll be stale again next quarter. Same investigation should cover the experiment we haven't run — a frontier model with just our docs, against the Wizard, and where the gaps actually are.

### 1. Self-driving first experience

Flip the problem on its head. What do you want to know about your business? 

We suggest questions and we suggest scouts based on their code base. We instrument to be able to answer questions. 

We don't blindly assume, we help them get right to what they want to know by suggesting the possible end result, questions we can help them answer. We design event captures and connect signals based on this information. 

### 2. Customizable, modular setup

If the experience above is us suggesting the questions worth answering, this is how the user steers it. Everything we suggest is a choice, and the setup is assembled from what they tick — not from a fixed script we run at everyone.

We ask people what they want and then we don't honor it. That's the gap today. In 2025 that was the right call — users didn't expect or understand an agent driven experience, and skipping the questions got them off an empty page faster. That's no longer true.

The flow:

- (Agent) We scan your repo and come back with what we found: projects, and the questions about your business we think we can answer (10s)
    - Mission critical paths like checkout success rates, we can suggest scouts that alert you when they drop and suggest fixes.
    - Signup flow in `lib/auth.ts` — "where does signup silently fail?" — a scout watching signup → activation, filing replay evidence with each finding.
    - Stripe webhooks — "which failed payments become churn?" — connect it as a warehouse source.
    - `openai` imports — "what does AI cost per user, and where does it fail?" — a cost-spike and failed-generation scout.
- (User) You pick the projects, and you tick the questions that actually matter to you
- (Agent) Plans the flow from your picks and enqueues agents (15s)
- (Agent) We add and initialize the SDKs and hook up environment variables (30s - 1 min)
    - At this point, you can just leave and you'll have a working setup to continue with your agent. You _can_ just leave.
- (User) While that runs, we surface the rest of the suggestions — each one traceable to something we saw in your repo:
  - Which of these questions are you trying to answer? (we list what we inferred, they tick)
  - What's required to answer these questions?
    - Set up groups?
    - Cohorts?
    - How do we identify?
    - What features need to be enabled?
    - Where do we need explicit custom capture?
- (Agent) We instrument what your questions require to answer — every capture exists because it feeds a question or scout you ticked (3 minutes)
- (Agent) Then we clean up after ourselves, review our changes, and leave context for your agent. This can take 5+ minutes. That's fine, leave us running, we'll handle the rest.

**Every one of these is a checkbox. Always. Never a freeform prompt — those are evil.** Chatbot is the wrong vision. A first time user doesn't know what to type at us, and a text box is an invitation to get it wrong. It should be a highly scrutinized question that we ask and offer: here's a set of things I think you should care about, select all that apply.

That also means we never have a user's free text steering an agent that has write access to their repo, which is a security property I'd like to keep for free.

### 3. Our edge is not the model

We're not going to win on model quality and we don't need to. What a bare Fable can't do:

- **Self-driving know-how.** We're introducing a novel way to think about building products. This is context a model does not have. We are the first contact point to help them build a mental model of self-driving.
- **Auth.** We're logged in. We can mint the project token, wire the account, pre-populate the notebook. Every little handoff after signup makes a difference for a brand new account, and a model with our docs can only write code.
- **Free inference.** We pay for the tokens. They don't.
- **Running the app.** See above — this is the actual capability gap, in both directions.
- **The experience.** We're good at shows. That's not decoration, it's the part that doesn't converge.


### 4. Higher instrumentation quality

What do you care about?

Yes you, the user.

The V2 answer was to guess. We scanned the repo and wrote fifteen events we thought looked important. Many of them were kinda bad. They're only useful if we suggested them and the user actually wanted them.

The V3 answer inverts it: **suggest the scout first, and the instrumentation required for that scout becomes obvious.** We're not shipping `clicked sign up` because it's an event. We're shipping it because there's a scout watching signup abandonment and it needs that event to work. Every capture has a reason attached, and the reason is one the user already agreed to.

@sarahxsanders framed the front half of this better than I did, and I want it in here: the deliverable isn't an instrumentation, it's **the feeling of being known**. Scan the repo, produce evidence-backed claims about their _business_ rather than about their events, then present each claim back for the user to confirm, correct, or steer. Every correction is itself a signal, and those corrections are worth more than anything we can get from rooting through the repo alone. Then the claims map to the tools that serve them — "your activation looks like publishing an article" becomes "where does the publish flow silently fail, and here's the error tracking and session replay we'd turn on for that form."

Have no opinions? We'll scan your repo, propose the scouts and the captures they need, and you tick the ones that look right.

Have opinions? You mainly care about sign up conversion and time to checkout? We can do that for you, too.

We'll finish with a GPT 5.6 Sol class audit. This, alone, turns 4/11 projects capturing successfully post instrumentation after a wizard run to 11/11. In our tests, this gives us 13% higher ingestion rate after a Wizard run in the same period.

We can do this because GPT 5.6 Sol class models are faster, cheaper, and more capable than anything practical before.
